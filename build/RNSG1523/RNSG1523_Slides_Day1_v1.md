## Reproductive Foundations and Preconception

## Slide 1 - Reproductive cycles are a timeline, not a single day
- Day 1: reproductive foundations and preconception
- We will move from normal physiology to safe counseling.
- Clinical judgment thread: recognize cues, then generate solutions.

> NOTES: Talk track: Preview the progression from physiology to person-centered care. Ask: "Which topic today is most likely to become a clinical judgment item?" Common error: treating reproductive health as a list of isolated facts.

## Slide 2 - These five objectives define today's work
1. Review female reproductive A&P and menstrual-cycle phases.
2. Differentiate primary from secondary dysmenorrhea and nursing management.
3. Describe menopause changes, risk profile, and health-promotion priorities.
4. Compare contraceptive methods by mechanism, effectiveness, contraindications, and teaching.
5. Explain preconception counseling and prenatal genetic carrier screening.

> NOTES: Talk track: Read the objectives exactly as written in the lesson plan. Ask students to mark the objective they expect to find hardest. Common error: memorizing methods without linking them to patient goals and contraindications. [Lowdermilk] [ACOG CO 760] [CDC US MEC 2024] [USPSTF 2023]

## Slide 4 - The opening poll separates facts from reasoning
- Poll: ovulation always occurs on day 14.
- Poll: severe menstrual pain in an adolescent is expected.
- Poll: condoms are interchangeable with every other contraceptive method.
- Poll: a positive carrier screen diagnoses disease.

> NOTES: Talk track: Collect answers without correcting yet. Ask students to explain what data would change their answer. Common error: answering from a memorized slogan instead of identifying the missing cue. [Lowdermilk] [ACOG CO 760] [CDC US MEC 2024] [USPSTF 2023]

## Slide 5 - Hormonal signaling coordinates two linked cycles
- GnRH stimulates pituitary FSH and LH release.
- Follicular development raises estradiol.
- The LH surge triggers ovulation.
- The luteal phase is progesterone-dominant.
- The uterine cycle moves menstrual -> proliferative -> secretory.

> NOTES: Talk track: Draw both cycles on one timeline. Ask: "What is the cue that ovulation is approaching?" Common error: treating calendar day 14 as a physiologic rule. [Lowdermilk] [Silbert-Flagg]

## Slide 6 - Ovarian and uterine phases answer different questions
| Cycle | Phases | What the nurse connects |
|---|---|---|
| Ovarian | follicular -> ovulation -> luteal | follicle and hormone pattern |
| Uterine | menstrual -> proliferative -> secretory | endometrial response |
| Shared anchor | LH surge | ovulation timing |

> NOTES: Talk track: Have students point to the phase in which the endometrium is prepared for implantation. Ask: "What makes the two cycles related but not identical?" Common error: swapping ovarian and uterine labels. [Lowdermilk] [Silbert-Flagg]

## Slide 7 - The calendar is an estimate, not the cue itself
- Cycle length varies across people and across months.
- Ovulation timing can shift with illness, stress, lactation, and hormonal changes.
- Ask about the individual's pattern before interpreting a date.
- Use the patient's history and the clinical question together.

> NOTES: Talk track: Return to the poll. Ask: "What assessment question prevents a calendar-based error?" Common error: assuming every patient has a 28-day cycle. [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 8 - Dysmenorrhea becomes a clinical judgment problem when the pattern changes
- Primary dysmenorrhea has no identifiable pelvic pathology.
- Secondary dysmenorrhea has an underlying pelvic cause.
- New, worsening, persistent, or function-limiting pain needs further assessment.
- The nurse asks about timing, severity, function, and response to treatment.

> NOTES: Talk track: Emphasize the pattern, not the label. Ask: "Which cue would make you stop normalizing the pain?" Common error: assuming adolescent pain is automatically primary. [ACOG CO 760] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 9 - Primary and secondary dysmenorrhea have different cue patterns
| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | 6-12 months after menarche | later or newly worsening |
| Timing | with or just before flow | may precede flow or persist |
| Cause | prostaglandin-mediated contractions | pelvic pathology |
| Examples | no structural cause identified | endometriosis, fibroids, adenomyosis, PID |

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to defend which row is most discriminating. Common error: using the presence of pain alone to classify the condition. [ACOG CO 760] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 10 - NSAIDs work best when timed to prostaglandin activity
- Start an NSAID at or just before expected pain when appropriate.
- Heat and exercise can support symptom relief.
- Hormonal suppression is an option when aligned with patient goals.
- Reassess function and response rather than assuming treatment worked.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "What would you assess before recommending an NSAID?" Common error: teaching medication without checking contraindications or adherence. [ACOG CO 760] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 11 - Persistent pain is a referral cue, not a character flaw
- Investigate secondary causes when appropriate therapy has not improved symptoms within 3-6 months.
- Ask adolescents directly about missed school, sports, work, and sleep.
- Validate pain; do not frame it as expected suffering.
- Coordinate evaluation when the pattern is atypical.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to write one validating sentence. Common error: waiting for the patient to volunteer the effect on daily life. [ACOG CO 760]

## Slide 12 - Active learning 1 turns three patients into a matching problem
- Patient A: adolescent wants confidentiality and reports severe pain.
- Patient B: age 38, smokes daily, wants pregnancy prevention.
- Patient C: 6 weeks postpartum and exclusively breastfeeding.
- Teams choose an assessment priority and a counseling pathway for each.

> NOTES: Talk track: Give teams 8 minutes, then ask them to cite the cue that drove the choice. Common error: recommending a method before clarifying goals, risk factors, and access. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Nguyen et al. 2024]

## Slide 13 - Debrief: the patient's goal and risk profile must travel together
- Confidentiality, smoking, postpartum status, and breastfeeding alter counseling.
- Person-centered counseling is nondirective and evidence-based.
- A method is not "best" outside the patient's context.
- Reproductive coercion history requires safety-focused, private assessment.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask each team to name one alternative they rejected and why. Common error: equating highly effective with universally appropriate. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Nguyen et al. 2024]

## Slide 14 - Break: preserve the clinical judgment map
- Before break, name the cue that changed each plan.
- After break, we move from pain and physiology to prevention and risk reduction.
- Keep the thread map visible.

> NOTES: Talk track: Use the break to collect unanswered questions. Common error: treating the second half as unrelated content. [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 15 - Menopause is diagnosed by twelve months of amenorrhea without another cause
- Menopause is a retrospective clinical milestone.
- Perimenopause is the transition with changing cycles and symptoms.
- Postmenopause follows the final menstrual period.
- Interpret symptoms within age, history, and alternative-cause assessment.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "Which part is a diagnosis and which part is a transition?" Common error: assigning menopause from one missed period. [NAMS 2022] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 16 - Vasomotor and genitourinary symptoms affect function
- Hot flashes and night sweats can disrupt sleep and work.
- Vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and urinary urgency may cluster.
- Ask permission before discussing intimate symptoms.
- Screen for the patient's priorities rather than assuming the chief complaint.

> NOTES: Talk track: Model neutral language. Ask: "What would make this assessment culturally safe?" Common error: discussing menopause only as a hormone value. [NAMS 2022] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 17 - Menopause changes the prevention conversation
| Risk area | Prevention focus |
|---|---|
| Bone loss | resistance/weight-bearing activity; calcium/vitamin D plan |
| Cardiovascular risk | blood pressure, lipids, smoking cessation, activity |
| Sleep and mood | sleep hygiene; assess distress and support |
| Genitourinary symptoms | symptom-directed care and follow-up |

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask which prevention action is most immediately actionable for a given patient. Common error: presenting risk as destiny instead of a target for prevention. [NAMS 2022] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 18 - Hormone therapy requires individualized shared decision-making
- Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and GSM.
- The benefit-risk ratio is more favorable for many symptomatic patients under 60 or within 10 years of menopause onset without contraindications.
- Reassess periodically; do not use a one-size-fits-all script.
- The nurse provides accurate information and advocacy, not persuasion.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "What information must be shared before a patient can decide?" Common error: treating hormone therapy as universally safe or universally unsafe. [NAMS 2022]

## Slide 19 - Contraceptive counseling compares mechanisms and priorities
| Method group | Examples | Core mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier | condoms, diaphragm | physical/chemical barrier |
| Combined hormonal | pill, patch, ring | ovulation suppression plus mucus change |
| Progestin-only | POP, DMPA, implant | mucus, endometrium, +/- ovulation |
| LARC | LNG-IUD, Cu-IUD, implant | long-acting reversible prevention |
| Permanent | salpingectomy, vasectomy | occlusion or removal |

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to compare mechanism with user-dependent adherence. Common error: assuming mechanism predicts typical-use effectiveness by itself. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 20 - U.S. MEC categories convert patient conditions into eligibility guidance
- Category 1: no restriction for use.
- Category 2: advantages generally outweigh risks.
- Category 3: risks usually outweigh advantages.
- Category 4: unacceptable health risk.
- Apply the category to the method-condition pair, not to the patient as a whole.

> NOTES: Talk track: Demonstrate how the same condition can produce different recommendations for different methods. Common error: calling a category a universal prohibition. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Nguyen et al. 2024]

## Slide 21 - ACHES converts estrogen safety teaching into memorable cues
- A: abdominal pain that may be severe
- C: chest pain or shortness of breath
- H: headaches that are severe or unusual
- E: eye or vision changes
- S: severe leg pain or swelling

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to add the action: stop and seek urgent evaluation per the clinical plan. Common error: teaching the mnemonic without the reason for urgency. [CDC US MEC 2024]

## Slide 22 - PATCH focuses IUD danger signs and follow-up
- P: period late or abnormal pregnancy concern
- A: abdominal pain
- T: temperature or fever
- C: chills or unusual discharge concern
- H: heavy bleeding or string concern

> NOTES: Talk track: Explain that local teaching materials must match the department's adopted mnemonic and protocol. Ask: "Which finding changes urgency?" Common error: treating strings as a self-diagnosis rather than a reason to contact the clinic. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 23 - Contraceptive access is part of clinical safety
- Ask about cost, pharmacy access, transportation, insurance, and privacy.
- Condoms reduce STI transmission; most other methods do not.
- Respect cultural or religious preferences while explaining options accurately.
- Screen privately for reproductive coercion and safety concerns.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "What is the nursing action when a patient cannot safely store medication at home?" Common error: labeling an access barrier as nonadherence. [CDC US MEC 2024] [Nguyen et al. 2024]

## Slide 24 - Preconception care moves prevention before pregnancy
- Review chronic disease control, medications, immunizations, and substance use.
- Identify teratogenic exposures before conception when possible.
- Assess family history, pedigree, and prior pregnancy outcomes.
- Coordinate referrals rather than asking the patient to manage the system alone.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "Which part of this assessment prevents harm before a pregnancy is recognized?" Common error: waiting until the first prenatal visit to begin risk reduction. [USPSTF 2023] [ACOG 2017] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 25 - Folic acid is a preconception intervention
- USPSTF gives an A recommendation for daily folic acid supplementation.
- Typical counseling range: 0.4-0.8 mg (400-800 mcg) daily.
- Neural tube formation occurs before many pregnancies are recognized.
- Link the recommendation to access, adherence, and patient understanding.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to explain why timing matters. Common error: presenting folic acid as a treatment after a neural tube defect has formed. [USPSTF 2023]

## Slide 26 - Carrier screening changes reproductive information, not certainty
- Use family history and a pedigree to identify questions.
- Expanded or ethnicity-informed carrier screening may be offered.
- A positive carrier screen usually identifies carrier status; it does not diagnose an affected fetus.
- Counseling and informed choice follow the result.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "What is the next nursing action after a positive screen?" Common error: using the word diagnosis for a screening result. [ACOG PB 226] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 27 - Informatics makes family history actionable
- Use the EHR problem list and structured family-history fields.
- Document the patient's words and the relationship of affected relatives.
- Use current evidence resources at the point of care.
- Protect privacy when genetic information is shared.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to identify one documentation detail that changes referral. Common error: entering a vague "family history positive" without a relationship or condition. [ACOG PB 226] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 28 - First action: choose the safest next counseling move
Scenario: A 16-year-old requests contraception, reports severe new pelvic pain, and asks that no one be told.

A. Recommend an IUD immediately.
B. Assess safety, pain severity, and urgent red flags in private.
C. Tell the patient to return with a parent.
D. Reassure the patient that adolescent pain is expected.

**Answer: B.** It establishes urgency and a safe, person-centered assessment.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask students to name the data still needed. Common error: jumping to a method before triage. [ACOG CO 760] [CDC US MEC 2024]

## Slide 29 - The other three options fail for different reasons
- A fails: method selection follows assessment and preference clarification.
- C fails: do not state local minor-consent rules from memory; verify with policy/legal counsel.
- D fails: new or worsening function-limiting pain is a cue for evaluation.
- Transferable principle: assess urgency and context before generating a solution.

> NOTES: Talk track: Make the distinction between clinical assessment and local legal policy explicit. Ask: "Which part belongs in a VERIFY note?" Common error: confusing a legal assumption with a clinical fact. [ACOG CO 760] [CDC US MEC 2024] [VERIFY: Texas minors' consent and confidentiality rules - local policy/legal counsel]

## Slide 30 - Active learning 2: build a bowtie from worsening pain
- Center condition: persistent dysmenorrhea despite appropriate NSAID use.
- Choose two actions: focused assessment, follow-up/referral, reassurance, or medication escalation without assessment.
- Monitor two parameters: function and pain pattern/response.
- State the condition and explain why each distractor fails.

> NOTES: Talk track: Give pairs 6 minutes. Ask them to use data, not labels. Common error: selecting a treatment without assessing for secondary causes. [ACOG CO 760] [Lowdermilk]

## Slide 31 - The bowtie principle is reusable across the course
- Recognize the pattern change.
- Analyze the impact on function and safety.
- Generate a patient-aligned plan.
- Evaluate response and escalate when the pattern persists.

> NOTES: Talk track: Connect the bowtie to the NCJMM operations used in later sessions. Common error: stopping after an intervention instead of evaluating outcomes. [ACOG CO 760]

## Slide 32 - Ethical care protects autonomy and access
- Counseling should be accurate, nondirective, and free of coercion.
- Patients may accept or decline screening and contraception after counseling.
- Use interpreters and accessible materials when indicated.
- Texas statutory content is not stated here; route it to local compliance/legal verification.

> NOTES: Talk track: Ask: "What would advocacy sound like without persuasion?" Common error: assuming the nurse's preferred method is the patient's best method. [ACOG PB 226] [CDC US MEC 2024] [VERIFY: Texas statutory content and minors' consent/confidentiality - local compliance/legal counsel]
